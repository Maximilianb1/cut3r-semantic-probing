# Is the capacity gap uniform, or concentrated in a few categories?

Working notes behind EXP-007's "Interpretation" section. Generated by
`python -m src.segmentation.analysis.build_probe_capacity_comparison`, run
against the expanded-training MLP results (EXP-006) and this
EXP-007's linear-probe results. Figures:
`probe-capacity-comparison.png` (aggregate) and
`probe-capacity-per-category.png` (per-category scatter), both in this
folder. This file is the detailed per-category table behind that scatter;
EXP-007 keeps only the headline.

## Setup

For each backbone, per-category test macro-IoU under the `[512]` MLP head
(from EXP-006) vs. the linear (`[]`) head (EXP-007), same 101
test windows, same 26 categories. `delta = mlp - linear`; a large positive
delta means that category needs the MLP's nonlinear capacity to score well.

## Result: CUT3R-trained and DINOv2 are broadly capacity-invariant; CUT3R-random is not

**CUT3R-trained** — no category has a gap over 0.2. The single largest gap
is `ball` (+0.189); every other category is within 0.14 of parity, and four
categories actually score *higher* under the linear head (`frisbee`,
`microwave`, `carrot`, `book`, `parkingmeter` — noise at this sample size, ~4
test windows per category, not a real linear-probe advantage). The 26
per-category deltas have mean +0.037 (matching the aggregate number) and a
tight spread — consistent with a representation where the segmentation
signal is close to linearly readable everywhere, not just on a few easy
categories.

**DINOv2** — three categories cross the 0.2 threshold: `stopsign` (+0.241),
`bench` (+0.209), `toyplane` (+0.200, right at the line). Everything else is
under 0.14, and two categories (`ball`, `wineglass`) score marginally higher
under the linear head. A slightly wider spread than CUT3R-trained, but still
overwhelmingly a small, near-uniform tax — no category collapses.

**CUT3R-random** — six categories cross the 0.2 threshold, several by a wide
margin:

| Category | MLP `[512]` | Linear `[]` | Delta |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| carrot | 0.542 | 0.080 | +0.463 |
| ball | 0.647 | 0.264 | +0.383 |
| plant | 0.557 | 0.185 | +0.372 |
| sandwich | 0.298 | 0.000 | +0.298 |
| cake | 0.366 | 0.089 | +0.277 |
| chair | 0.283 | 0.009 | +0.275 |

Three of these (`sandwich`, `chair`, and near-zero already for `bowl`,
`microwave`) go to a linear-head IoU of essentially 0 — the linear probe
finds *nothing* on categories where the MLP head still recovered a
middling-to-decent score. This is a materially different pattern from the
other two backbones: it is not a uniform tax, it is specific categories
losing (almost) all their signal the moment the head loses nonlinear
capacity.

## What this teaches

The aggregate bar chart alone (macro-IoU dropping 4.8% / 8.4% / 42.2%
relative for CUT3R-trained / DINOv2 / CUT3R-random) could in principle be
explained either by "every category gets slightly worse" or by "a handful
of categories collapse while the rest are unaffected." The per-category
breakdown resolves this in favor of **different mechanisms for different
backbones**:

- CUT3R-trained's and DINOv2's aggregate drops are genuinely distributed
  evenly across categories — reinforcing the EXP-007 conclusion that their
  segmentation signal is broadly, not selectively, linearly accessible.
- CUT3R-random's drop is concentrated: a subset of categories
  (carrot/ball/plant/sandwich/cake/chair) apparently score reasonably under
  the MLP only because the head's nonlinear capacity is compensating for a
  representation that, for those specific object types, carries little to
  no linearly separable foreground/background signal. The remaining
  categories were already near-floor under the MLP head (e.g. `bowl`
  0.018, `microwave` 0.006) and stay there under the linear head too — there
  is no "collapse" to observe because there was nothing there to begin with.

**Open question, not resolved here:** whether the six
collapsing-under-linear categories share a visual property (carrot, cake,
sandwich are food items with soft/organic silhouettes; ball, plant, chair
are not) is not obvious from the category labels alone. A qualitative look
at CUT3R-random's actual predictions on these categories (parallel to
EXP-006's `build_delta_comparison_plot.py` treatment of CUT3R-trained vs
DINOv2) is the natural follow-up, flagged as a next action in EXP-007 rather
than pursued here.
